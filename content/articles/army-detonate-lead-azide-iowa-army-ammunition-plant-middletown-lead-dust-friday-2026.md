The US Army plans to blow up about 5 tons of unstable explosives at an ammunition plant in southeast Iowa on Friday, and people living downwind are worried about what will come down after the blast.

The Army will carry out two controlled detonations of 68 barrels of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown around noon Central Time on Friday, Nov. 20. The blasts will send up a plume of dust containing lead, a toxic metal, that could settle on farmland, playgrounds and homes across parts of eastern Iowa and northwest Illinois.

The Army told CBS News Chicago that operations like this near populated communities are highly uncommon.

## Why the Army is blowing it up

Lead azide is a highly sensitive explosive used in military ammunition since World War I. The barrels had been stored at the plant for more than 20 years in earthen-covered containers, sitting in a bath of ethanol and water to keep the material stable, said Justine Barati, a public affairs specialist at the Army Sustainment Command, according to the Associated Press.

During a routine check earlier this year, workers found that some of the water had evaporated, causing the lead azide to crystallize. That makes it more sensitive to friction and shock, "creating a highly unstable and dangerous situation," according to the emergency permit approved by the Environmental Protection Agency.

Officials said moving the drums posed an unacceptable risk of an accidental explosion, and that the risk grows in cold weather. Alternatives, such as neutralizing the material with other chemicals, were considered and rejected because any plan that involved handling the drums would raise the chance of a fatal blast.

"We're really concerned that it will detonate on its own," Barati said.

## Who has to leave

The Army is asking people in designated areas near Middletown City Park and Butterfly Park to leave their homes from 8 a.m. to 2 p.m. CT on Friday, because ground vibrations from the blasts could break windows. Temporary accommodations are available at the Armed Forces Reserve Center in Middletown, a town of nearly 400 people.

Several school districts in southeast Iowa have canceled classes on Friday. People in the plume area who cannot leave are advised to stay inside and keep windows and doors closed.

A siren will sound short blasts five minutes before the detonation and one long blast at one minute, Army officials told WQAD. They said not to expect a "massive Hollywood fireball," but a brief flash, a steam plume and dust.

## Where the lead dust could go

The dust is expected to linger for no more than an hour, depending on wind speed, officials said. Army models show elevated lead particles in the air across an area up to 20 miles wide, stretching from Burlington to well north of the Quad Cities. Lead particles could also settle on the ground in an area up to 10 miles wide, from near Burlington to north of Wapello and New Boston, NPR Illinois reported.

Forecasts show gusty southerly winds of 20 to 30 mph on Friday carrying the plume north through eastern Iowa, well west of the Chicago area, CBS News Chicago reported. The Army will update its maps 48 hours before the detonation, six hours before and every hour after.

The Army says airborne lead levels are expected to stay well below the level at which the EPA would require protective action. To limit the spread, crews will surround the site with sand piles, spray the soil with water and mist the air.

## The health concern

According to the EPA, no safe level of lead in children has been identified.

Patrick O'Shaughnessy, a professor of occupational and environmental health at the University of Iowa, told the AP that it is encouraging to see the Army and EPA working to minimize the dust, but it is still unknown how much will end up in the air and on surfaces. Lead is a neurological toxicant that can harm brain development, especially in young children, he said, adding that he is particularly concerned about toddlers who put things in their mouths.

After the blasts, the Army recommends washing cars and cleaning windowsills, children's play surfaces and other outdoor surfaces. Under the permit, soil and surface sampling must begin within 24 hours in affected areas, and the Army must clean up soil lead above regulatory limits. Residents can sign up for free voluntary sampling.

## Late notice and protests

Middletown residents learned in March that the Army needed to dispose of the barrels. The EPA approved an emergency permit on Nov. 13, which required five days' public notice. That led to notifications on Sunday to communities as far as 100 miles away. Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds' office said it was first briefed on Monday.

On Wednesday, protesters gathered outside the Armed Forces Reserve Center in Middletown, calling for more information and a delay. Illinois environmental and public health officials have urged the military to carry out full sampling in their state after the blasts.

The plant has a history with explosions. In December 1941, a major blast at what was then the Iowa Ordnance Plant destroyed buildings at the site.
