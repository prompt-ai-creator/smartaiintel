## Update: Carney and Trump meet for about 20 minutes, agree to keep talking

Carney and Trump met on Wednesday evening in Shenzhen, their first in-person meeting since last year. The conversation lasted just under 20 minutes, Carney's office said. It took place at dinnertime in Shenzhen, early morning in Canada.

"The leaders discussed the work underway to reach a mutually beneficial trade agreement that would create good jobs, boost affordability, and fully respect Canada's languages, culture, and sovereignty," Carney's office said in a readout. "They agreed to continue the work and to stay in close contact."

No deal was announced, and the readout gave no details on tariffs. The White House has not released its own account of the meeting.

Trump was joined by US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and his chief of staff, according to The Canadian Press. Carney was joined by Foreign Affairs Minister Anita Anand and his chief of staff, Marc-André Blanchard. With both lead US trade negotiators in the room, the meeting focused on trade.

Before the meeting, Carney told reporters that trade talks with Washington are "much more complex" than those with Beijing.

Carney also rejected claims Russian President Vladimir Putin made at the summit that Western sanctions no longer matter. "Not for the first time Vladimir Putin is lying about the situation on the ground," Carney said. "Sanctions are very real and they're going to continue to increase as long as this war goes on."

The original preview, published before the meeting, follows below.

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney says he will meet President Donald Trump on Wednesday in Shenzhen, China, in an unscheduled meeting that could test whether the two neighbors can start to end their trade war.

Carney announced the meeting to reporters on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit. Until Wednesday, his aides had said there was no expectation of a meeting with Trump in China, The New York Times reported. There was no immediate confirmation from the US side.

Carney said he still believes a deal that benefits both countries is possible. "We will do what's necessary in order to achieve that deal, but only if that deal is in the interest of Canadians."

He described talks with the Trump administration as "a two steps forward, one step back type thing."

## What's on the table

**Tariffs on autos, steel and aluminum.** Most Canadian goods that meet the rules of the Canada-US-Mexico Agreement (CUSMA) are effectively shielded from Trump's tariffs. But that protection does not cover Canada's key auto, steel and aluminum industries, which face tariffs of up to 50%.

After trade talks collapsed in August, Washington also put 50% tariffs on about US$20 billion of Canadian goods, and Ottawa hit back with its own counter-tariffs.

**A possible bilateral deal.** Last week in Manila, Carney said he sees a possible one-on-one deal to scale back US tariffs on steel, aluminum and autos. "We are making progress. There is the possibility of a deal, a bilateral deal, but we're not yet there," he said.

**The future of CUSMA.** In July, the US declined to renew the continental trade pact in its current form, starting a process of yearly reviews. The agreement stays in force for now, and it still shields about 90% of Canada's exports to the US.

**Canada's China deals.** Trump is likely to ask about Canada's growing trade with Beijing. On Tuesday, Carney and Chinese President Xi Jinping agreed that [China will drop tariffs on about C$400 million of Canadian pork and seafood](https://smartaiintel.com/articles/carney-meets-xi-shenzhen-apec-china-canada-relations-stable-2026/). In return, Canada extended exemptions for some Chinese steel and aluminum by two years and added 32 more items. In January, Canada also cut its tariff on Chinese electric vehicles from 100% to 6.1% for up to 49,000 cars a year.

Carney played down the issue on Wednesday, saying Canada is mainly selling farm and seafood products to China. "These are not cutting-edge semiconductors," he said. He added that for Trump, China is "not the top issue at all."

## The critics

**The White House.** Trump's trade adviser Peter Navarro attacked Carney this week after the prime minister suggested Canada could turn to Chinese automakers if talks with Washington fail.

"Are u really threatening to play China off the U.S.? This is offensive in the extreme," Navarro wrote. "You should be far more worried China is going to eat Canada alive." US Ambassador to Canada Pete Hoekstra reshared the post.

**Canada's steel industry.** Steel producers have warned that the new exemptions for Chinese steel could become a loophole that lets cheap Chinese steel flood the Canadian market, according to iPolitics. Liberal MP John-Paul Danko defended the deal.

**China skeptics.** Former diplomat Michael Kovrig, who was detained in China for more than 1,000 days, said that relations with Beijing are "not back to normal, and they shouldn't be." He added: "You have to put Washington first. I know that's not a popular idea in Canada right now."

"I think Mr. Carney is walking a very fine line," Carleton University business professor Ian Lee said.

**Opposition parties.** At home, Carney faces pressure over job losses in the auto sector. Conservative Leader Pierre Poilievre has called on Carney to fire Industry Minister Mélanie Joly over Stellantis's plant in Brampton, Ontario, where Jeep production plans were moved to Illinois. NDP Leader Avi Lewis said: "This tariff war is an attack on Canada and Donald Trump wants to de-industrialize this country."

## What's at stake

**Canada's auto industry.** Trump has threatened to raise tariffs on Canadian-made vehicles and auto parts to 50% on Jan. 1, 2027. The sector supports about 125,000 direct jobs, and more than 90% of Canadian-made vehicles are sold in the US. Carney has said that if talks fail, Canada could look to European, Japanese or Chinese automakers.

**Canada's trade shift.** Canada's exports to China rose 40% to C$33.5 billion in the first nine months of 2026. But the US remains by far Canada's largest trading partner, and analysts say China cannot replace the American market.

**US-Canada relations.** A breakthrough could ease a trade war that has hurt businesses on both sides of the border. A failure could push Canada closer to China, which would likely anger Washington ahead of the next CUSMA review.

At last year's APEC summit in South Korea, the two leaders spoke only informally at a dinner. How much progress they can make in a short meeting on Wednesday is unclear.
