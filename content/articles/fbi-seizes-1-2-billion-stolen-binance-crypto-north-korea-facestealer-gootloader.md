The U.S. Justice Department and the FBI unveiled a three-front cybercrime crackdown on Thursday, Nov. 12, 2026—securing court orders to seize more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, dismantling North Korea's $400 million "FaceStealer" webcam-hijacking infrastructure, and seizing U.S. command domains powering the Gootloader ransomware botnet.

## Key takeaways from Thursday's DOJ and FBI cyber takedowns

- **`$1.2 billion` Binance recovery:** Following the late-October theft of more than `$6 billion` from **Binance** attributed to North Korean military hackers (**TraderTraitor**), U.S. prosecutors and private-sector partners froze over **`$2.7 billion`** and secured federal court authorization to seize or restrain more than **`$1.2 billion`**.
- **`$400 million` "FaceStealer" takedown:** FBI Los Angeles agents seized cloud infrastructure and more than **`900` secretly recorded victim webcam videos** used by North Korean hacking groups (`APT38` / `Sapphire Sleet` / `BlueNoroff`) to steal over `$400 million` in crypto over the past 18 months.
- **Open-source supply-chain warning:** Federal investigators revealed North Korean operatives were increasingly aiming FaceStealer lures at **open-source software developers and repository maintainers** to plant malicious code downstream.
- **Gootloader botnet domain seizures:** In a parallel Operation Endgame action led out of Colorado with Europol and Army CID, federal warrants purged **Gootloader** malware from **`100+` compromised servers**—replacing criminal command domains with official DOJ/FBI seizure banners after the botnet infected `25,000+` computers and enabled `$28.8 million` in U.S. ransomware losses.

## How did North Korea's 'FaceStealer' hijack 900 webcams on fake video calls?

According to an FBI Cyber Division alert and an IC3 technical advisory released Thursday, FaceStealer exploits everyday trust in video-conferencing links. After compromising an initial victim in the Web3 or software sector, North Korean operatives silently record the person's webcam feed and hijack their messaging and social accounts to invite the victim's real-world colleagues onto an imposter video call.

When the next target joins the meeting, hackers play back the pre-recorded webcam video of their trusted colleague—or deploy AI-generated deepfake video of prominent crypto executives—before triggering a fake `"microphone cannot be heard"` error prompt. Clicking the supplied troubleshooting patch silently installs malware that drains digital wallets and harvests credentials months or even more than a year later. Assistant Director in Charge Patrick Grandy of the FBI's Los Angeles Field Office warned that even seasoned security engineers have fallen prey to the replay tactic, launching a dedicated federal portal (`fbi.gov/facestealer`) for victim checks.

## How fast are U.S. prosecutors freezing billion-dollar crypto heists?

Assistant Attorney General A. Tysen Duva of the Justice Department's Criminal Division and U.S. Attorney Jeanine Ferris Pirro for the District of Columbia credited rapid coordination with virtual asset service providers (VASPs), the U.S. Secret Service, and IRS Criminal Investigation for halting nearly half of the historic Oct. 29 Binance breach before TraderTraitor launderers could push the tokens through cross-chain bridges and mixers.

Combined with Thursday's [Operation TorNado dark-web raids seizing `$234 million` in cash and crypto](/articles/fbi-europol-operation-tornado-354-arrests-234-million-seized/) and ongoing [enterprise zero-day cybersecurity disclosures](/articles/microsoft-patches-1259-flaws-outlook-zero-day-railway-ai-key-breach/), the coordinated actions mark the largest single-day virtual-asset and botnet disruption announced by the Justice Department in 2026.
