France will raise its minimum wage by 1.6% from December 1, bringing the government’s stated net monthly amount to €1,502, Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu announced following talks with unions and employers.

The increase brings forward the wage adjustment as the government faces pressure to respond to rising living costs. For workers paid at the legal minimum, the announcement offers a specific amount and start date rather than another pledge to discuss purchasing power.

The [government’s account of the social conference](https://www.info.gouv.fr/actualite/conference-sociale-hausse-du-smic-de-1-6) says the package also includes plans for a wage monitoring committee and a higher ceiling for the fuel bonus. Its short summary does not give the new bonus ceiling or detailed eligibility rules.

That distinction matters for households trying to work out what they will receive. The minimum wage announcement contains a stated monthly benchmark, while the other measures still require more detail before their individual value can be calculated.

## A smaller increase than unions sought

The decision follows demands for a larger response. Before the outcome, [Reuters reported on the competing wage proposals](https://www.usnews.com/news/world/articles/2026-11-09/french-pm-lecornu-holds-talks-over-protesters-cost-of-living-concerns): the CFDT wanted a €100 net monthly increase, while the CGT sought a 10% rise.

Reuters put the existing net monthly minimum at about €1,478. Against that rounded figure, the newly announced €1,502 amounts to roughly €24 more a month. That comparison is approximate and is not a payroll calculation for every employee.

The gap between the government’s measure and the union demands helps explain why an increase does not, by itself, establish that the wider dispute is resolved. No agreement by both unions is recorded in the government’s short announcement.

## What workers should check next

The figure is a minimum wage benchmark, not a promise that every salary in France will increase by 1.6%. Workers earning above the minimum should not assume the same percentage will automatically appear in their pay.

Employees will need the implementing details and information from their employer to establish how the new rate applies to their working hours and payroll. The government’s summary gives a net monthly amount but does not set out a new gross hourly figure.

For now, the confirmed announcement is narrower than a general solution to the cost of living dispute: an earlier minimum wage adjustment from December 1, alongside further measures whose practical terms still need clarification.
