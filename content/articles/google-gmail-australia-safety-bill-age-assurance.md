Google has warned that Australia’s proposed online safety rules could force restrictions on teenagers using Gmail and other everyday internet services. The disputed provision requires age assurance and access controls where children face a foreseeable risk of encountering pornography, but does not expressly order email providers to ban everyone under 18.

The difference matters. Google is describing a possible consequence of compliance, not announcing a withdrawal of Gmail or reporting a ban already imposed by Parliament.

Rachel Lord, YouTube’s public policy manager, raised the concern during a Senate hearing, according to [the Sydney Morning Herald’s account of Google’s warning](https://www.smh.com.au/technology/teens-could-be-locked-out-of-gmail-under-safety-bill-google-warns-20261109-p61coy.html). The company argues that the legislation could reach email, messaging and storage services because those services can carry explicit material even when that is not their main purpose.

The government disputes the implication that children should lose ordinary internet access. A spokesperson for Communications Minister Anika Wells told the newspaper that cutting children off from email or the internet was not the intention.

## The clause behind the warning

The relevant language appears in proposed section 27D of the [bill as read a third time in the House of Representatives](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/search/display/display.w3p;query=Id%3A%22legislation%2Fbills%2Fr7553_third-reps%2F0002%22).

It applies when there is a reasonably foreseeable risk that a child could access, or be exposed to, pornography or material depicting specific fetish practices or fantasies through an online service.

Where technically feasible and reasonably practicable, the provider must implement a choice of at least two appropriate age assurance measures for users in Australia, together with access control measures.

That is more specific than a general demand to make services safer. It places an express obligation alongside the bill’s wider digital duty of care.

It also leaves an important implementation question: how should a service that mainly carries ordinary private communications prevent children encountering prohibited material without excluding them from the service itself?

The clause does not name Gmail. Nor does it expressly prescribe a blanket prohibition on email accounts for people under 18. Treating Google’s warning as an enacted Gmail ban would therefore go beyond the text.

## Age assurance is not a prescribed identity document

Section 27D requires a choice of appropriate age assurance measures. It does not itself prescribe one identity document that every Australian must submit.

That does not settle how individual companies would design their checks. The suitability of particular measures, their feasibility and the accompanying access controls would still matter.

For readers, the distinction is practical. A legal obligation to establish age, a requirement to restrict access to particular material and a decision to exclude a whole age group from a service are not interchangeable descriptions.

Google’s argument is that the obligation could push providers toward broader restrictions. The government’s stated objective is protection from harmful material, not removal of ordinary communications tools. Those positions concern the likely operation of the bill, rather than a shutdown already under way.

## Compliance has consequences, but implementation is not immediate

Proposed section 27E makes failure to comply with section 27D subject to a civil penalty provision. Section 27F would also let the eSafety Commissioner issue remedial directions where the regulator reasonably believes a provider has failed to implement the required measures.

The [government’s explanation of the proposed framework](https://www.infrastructure.gov.au/media-communications/internet/online-safety/digital-duty-care) describes a broader shift toward making services identify and address risks, rather than relying only on action after harmful material appears.

The commencement table in the [House third reading bill](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/download/legislation/bills/r7553_third-reps/toc_pdf/26131b01.pdf;fileType=application%2Fpdf) places the digital duty of care schedule on the day after the end of a 12 month period beginning with Royal Assent. That is a proposed implementation timetable, not evidence that these duties are already operating.

The issue to watch is whether Parliament changes the scope of the age assurance clause or clarifies how it should apply to general communications services. Until then, the accurate description is a dispute about the possible reach of proposed safety obligations, not an Australian ban on teenagers using Gmail.
