A federal judge in Miami has rejected a bid by the British Broadcasting Corporation to throw out President Donald Trump's multibillion-dollar defamation lawsuit over a 2024 *Panorama* documentary, clearing the core libel claim to advance into high-stakes pretrial discovery even as he dismissed a separate statutory count.

In a 40-page ruling issued in the US District Court for the Southern District of Florida, District Judge Jeffrey T. Kuntz concluded that Trump's legal team pleaded sufficient factual allegations to support a plausible inference of actual malice regarding how the British public broadcaster spliced together two passages delivered 55 minutes apart during Trump's January 6, 2021 Ellipse speech in Washington.

Trump had originally sought at least $5 billion on a defamation count and another $5 billion under Florida's Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act, putting the headline complaint at $10 billion. By dismissing the Florida consumer-protection count while preserving the $5 billion defamation claim, Kuntz kept the heart of the lawsuit alive — setting up an immediate legal test ahead of a court-ordered December 11 deadline for the sitting president to sit for a sworn deposition with BBC attorneys.

## Judge cites 55-minute gap in edited January 6 documentary sequence

At the center of the dispute is an episode of the BBC's flagship investigative program *Panorama* that aired roughly one week before the November 2024 US presidential election. Trump's complaint alleges that producers joined two separate portions of his January 6 remarks — omitting intervening passages in which he urged supporters to march peacefully and patriotically — in a manner that falsely portrayed him as directly inciting violence at the US Capitol.

While the BBC previously issued an apology over the editing error in London during internal fallout that led to senior editorial departures, the broadcaster urged the Miami federal court to dismiss the US civil suit at the threshold stage, arguing that its reporting was protected commentary on a matter of intense global public concern and that Trump — who went on to win the 2024 presidential election days later — suffered no cognizable legal injury.

Judge Kuntz disagreed that the case could be resolved on a motion to dismiss. Pointing specifically to the nearly hour-long separation between the two spliced statements in the original transcript, Kuntz wrote that "given the time disparity between the two statements, the distance itself tends toward a plausible inference that BBC knew or should have known that the segment they were creating would not and could not have been a truthful statement."

"This was not trimming around the edges but creating something different than what was stated in the speech," Kuntz wrote, while emphasizing that he was not ruling on the ultimate merits: "Whether President Trump can prove these claims and establish he sustained damages at summary judgment or trial is left for another day."

## Trump hails 'Huge Victory' as BBC vows vigorous defense

Writing on his Truth Social platform shortly after the order was docketed, Trump celebrated the decision as "A Huge Victory against Fake News BBC," pledging to continue pursuing litigation against media organizations he accuses of deceptive editing. The legal boost arrived on the same week the White House navigated intense Capitol Hill battles, including Tuesday's narrow procedural vote in which [House Republicans blocked an immediate floor vote on impeaching Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth](https://smartaiintel.com/articles/house-blocks-pete-hegseth-impeachment-211-204-judiciary-vote/).

In London, a BBC spokesperson said the corporation was "surprised by this decision" while emphasizing that it respected the US court's authority and noted that the Florida consumer-protection claim had been dismissed.

"We remain confident in our position and will continue to respond through the proper legal process," the BBC spokesperson said in a statement.

## December 11 sworn deposition deadline puts pressure on both sides

Although surviving a motion to dismiss gives plaintiffs significant leverage in media-defamation cases — opening the door to internal emails, editing logs, and producer testimony — proceeding into full discovery carries acute reciprocal risks for a sitting president.

On October 22, a federal magistrate judge in Miami sided with BBC defense lawyers who argued that Trump had resisted scheduling sworn questioning, ordering the president to appear for a deposition on or before December 11 unless both parties agree to extend the discovery calendar. The court warned that failing to comply with the deposition order could expose Trump to judicial sanctions, up to and including dismissal of the lawsuit itself.

First Amendment and media-law scholars note that at summary judgment or trial, Trump will still face the demanding constitutional standard established under *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, requiring clear and convincing proof of actual malice as well as concrete financial or reputational damages — a steep hurdle given his subsequent electoral victory, making the next four weeks before the December 11 deposition a pivotal window for either a negotiated settlement or an unprecedented courtroom clash between a US president and Britain's public broadcaster.
