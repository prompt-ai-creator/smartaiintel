NOAA released its updated winter outlook on Thursday morning, and the message is clear: a historically strong El Niño is set to split the country. Most of the northern U.S. is favored to be warmer than normal from December through February, while the South and much of the East Coast are favored to be wetter than normal.

The Climate Prediction Center issued the new outlook at 8:30 a.m. ET on Nov. 19. "The strong El Nino is likely to be the major influence on temperatures and precipitation this winter through next spring," the agency said.

## The big picture: warmer North, wetter South

The updated December-January-February outlook favors:

- **Warmer than normal** across central and eastern Alaska and most of the northern two-thirds of the Lower 48
- **Near-normal temperatures** for parts of the Southeast, including much of Florida and the area from southeastern Louisiana to southern Georgia
- **Wetter than normal** from southwestern Oregon and California through the Southwest, the central and southern Plains, the Gulf Coast, the Southeast, the Mid-Atlantic and much of the Northeast
- **Drier than normal** for parts of the Great Lakes, the northern High Plains, the Northern Rockies and mainland Alaska

NOAA said it has more confidence in the precipitation forecast than the temperature forecast this winter, because a strong El Niño brings a reliable wet pattern to much of the country.

## What changed since October

Compared with its last outlook on Oct. 15, NOAA **raised the odds of a wet winter** for California, the Desert Southwest and the East Coast, based on the latest computer model runs.

- Southern California and southwestern Arizona now have a **better than 60% chance** of above-normal precipitation.
- Florida, southern Georgia and the coastal Carolinas have a **better than 70% chance**, the highest in the country.
- The wetter-than-normal zone now reaches north into the western Corn Belt and up the West Coast to Oregon.

## Region by region

**Northeast and Great Lakes:** The odds of a warmer-than-normal winter top 50%. The Northeast also leans wetter, while parts of the Great Lakes lean drier. Milder air means more storms could fall as rain or mixed precipitation, especially near the coast.

**Mid-Atlantic and Southeast:** This is the wettest signal on the map. Temperatures lean near normal in the Southeast, which NOAA said is how the strongest recent El Niño winters played out there. That combination raises the risk of heavy rain events through late winter, when El Niño's effects usually peak in the region.

**Midwest and Plains:** The Upper Mississippi Valley is among the areas most likely to be warmer than normal. The central and southern Plains lean wetter, while the Northern Plains lean drier.

**West:** California and the Southwest have improved odds of a wet winter, which is good news for mountain snowpack and drought. The Pacific Northwest has the highest odds of warmth in the country, over 60% from northwestern Oregon and Washington north into southeastern Alaska. The Northern Rockies lean drier, with odds above 50%.

**Alaska:** Central to eastern Alaska leans warmer, and mainland Alaska is favored to be drier than normal.

## What it means for snow

NOAA does not forecast seasonal snowfall totals, because snow is generally not predictable more than about a week ahead. But the outlook gives some clues:

- **Fewer snow chances:** A warmer, drier northern tier usually means a less snowy winter for places like the Great Lakes, Upper Midwest, Northern Plains and Northern Rockies.
- **Better mountain snow:** A wetter California and Southwest favors bigger snowpack in the Sierra Nevada and the mountains of the Southwest.
- **The East Coast wild card:** Storms are favored along the East Coast, but a snowy winter there also needs cold air at the right time.

That is where NOAA flagged its main uncertainty. Forecasters said how much "high latitude blocking" sets up this winter could change the picture. The North Atlantic Oscillation turned negative in early November, and major models favor it staying negative heading into December. That pattern tends to bring colder weather to the Southeast.

NOAA also pointed to the 2009-10 El Niño winter, when a strongly negative Arctic Oscillation brought below-normal temperatures across the Great Plains and Southeast. That winter also brought record snowstorms to the Mid-Atlantic. NOAA said a repeat "can not be completely ruled out" but called it the least likely outcome among recent strong El Niños.

## How strong is this El Niño?

NOAA's Nov. 12 update said a strong-to-very strong El Niño is likely to last through January-March 2027, with about a 60% chance it becomes the strongest on record since 1950.

As of mid-November, sea surface temperatures in the key Niño 3.4 region of the Pacific were 2.2 degrees Celsius above average. The agency's relative index for August through October was +2.0 degrees, just short of the +2.1 recorded during the famous 1997 event. NOAA expects El Niño to peak this winter, fade in the spring, and possibly give way to La Niña by fall 2027.

## When is the next update?

NOAA's next seasonal outlook is due **Dec. 17, 2026**. The outlooks show the odds for a whole three-month period, not day-to-day weather, so local forecasts should be used for specific storms and travel plans.
