NEW YORK — SpaceX and Tesla are advancing private negotiations toward a blockbuster corporate merger that could be announced as soon as December, according to reporting from Bloomberg News and Reuters citing people familiar with the discussions, setting the stage for what would be the largest deal in business history.

Independent special committees established by both boards in October are now working with financial and legal advisers to hammer out an exchange ratio and post-merger governance structure, moving the talks well past preliminary brainstorming. Sources cautioned that no final agreement has been signed and that the announcement timeline could slip into early 2027 if complex regulatory questions take longer to settle.

## A $3.4 trillion industrial and AI colossus

At current market values, uniting Elon Musk's two flagship public companies would eclipse every previous corporate transaction by an order of magnitude. SpaceX carries a market valuation above $1.9 trillion under its Nasdaq ticker SPCX following its blockbuster June initial public offering and earlier $250 billion acquisition of xAI, while Tesla trades near $1.5 trillion, giving the combined enterprise a paper value of roughly $3.4 trillion.

By comparison, the largest single merger in corporate history remains Vodafone's $183 billion takeover of German telecom group Mannesmann in 2000. Shares of Tesla climbed nearly 2 percent following Thursday's report as Wall Street weighed how many SpaceX shares Tesla investors would receive in an all-stock combination.

People familiar with the strategy said artificial intelligence sits at the center of the industrial pitch. Folded under a single corporate roof, Tesla's autonomous Cybercab fleet, Optimus humanoid robotics division, and utility-scale battery business would share custom silicon, orbital Starlink connectivity, and computing infrastructure directly with [SpaceX's newly unveiled $74 billion supercomputing campuses in Texas and Arizona](/articles/spacex-74-billion-supercompute-campuses-starbase-texas-cochise-county-arizona/).

## The China factory hurdle and Pentagon ties

Despite overlapping engineering ambitions, bankers and legal teams face a formidable geopolitical puzzle: how to ring-fence Tesla's sprawling Chinese manufacturing operations from SpaceX's sensitive U.S. national-security contracts.

Tesla's Gigafactory in Shanghai produces more than half of the automaker's global vehicle output, shipping 67,829 cars in October alone. SpaceX, meanwhile, launches classified spy satellites for the Pentagon and U.S. intelligence agencies and operates encrypted Starshield networks used by defense partners. JPMorgan analyst Rajat Gupta previously warned investors that SpaceX's defense work and Tesla's reliance on Beijing represent competing pressures that regulators in both capitals will scrutinize closely.

Corporate governance presents an equally delicate balancing act for the two special committees. Musk holds roughly 20 percent of Tesla under a standard one-share, one-vote structure, whereas dual-class super-voting stock gives him approximately 85 percent of voting power at SpaceX. Because Musk stands on both sides of any transaction, he told investors during SpaceX's October 20 earnings call that he has recused himself from the independent committee negotiations.

## What investors are watching in December

The two companies already share growing financial ties, including joint semiconductor packaging work and Tesla's $2 billion purchase of SpaceX Class A stock completed in March. Quarterly filings also show the pair hold a combined 30,221 bitcoin worth roughly $2.1 billion on their corporate balance sheets.

Tesla is scheduled to convene its annual shareholder meeting at Giga Texas on December 3, where Musk, board chair Robyn Denholm, and co-founder JB Straubel are up for board re-election. While Tesla's October proxy statement does not include a merger ballot item for that December 3 gathering, any agreement struck by the two special committees in the weeks ahead would set up a separate high-stakes shareholder vote and antitrust review in Washington, where incoming Democratic committee chairs following [last week's Senate flip](/articles/scott-colom-unseats-cindy-hyde-smith-mississippi-senate-55-democrat-majority/) are expected to examine major technology consolidations closely.
