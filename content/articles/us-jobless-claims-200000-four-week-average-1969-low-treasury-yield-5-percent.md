U.S. initial jobless claims rose by 9,000 to a seasonally adjusted 200,000 in the week ended November 7, the Department of Labor reported Thursday morning, while the four-week moving average held unchanged at 195,250 — matching its lowest level since October 1969.

Economists surveyed ahead of the `8:30 a.m. ET` release had forecast 194,000 to 195,000 first-time applications, following an upward revision of 2,000 to the prior week's total (`191,000`, up from an initially reported `189,000`). Even with Thursday's uptick — the largest single-week rise since August 8 — initial filings have now printed at or below the 200,000 threshold for nine consecutive weeks and have not topped 230,000 in any week of 2026.

- **Initial jobless claims (week ended Nov. 7):** `200,000` (`+9,000` week-over-week vs. `195,000` consensus; prior week revised to `191,000`).
- **Four-week initial claims average:** `195,250` (unchanged; matches lowest reading since October 1969 and sits `31,750` below a year ago).
- **Continuing claims (week ended Oct. 31):** `1,697,000` (`-20,000` from prior week's revised `1,717,000`, hovering just above a multi-year low of `1,695,000`).
- **Treasury yields after release:** Benchmark 10-year U.S. Treasury yield climbed past `5.006%`, with the 30-year yield near `5.358%` ahead of Thursday afternoon's long-bond auction.

## What do the November 12 US jobless claims numbers show?

Beneath the modest weekly bounce in first-time filings, continuing unemployment claims — which track Americans already receiving benefits and lag by one week — dropped by 20,000 to 1,697,000 in the week ended October 31, sitting near the lowest mark since April 2023. The four-week average of continuing claims edged down 1,500 to 1,707,750, remaining lower year-over-year for a 41st straight week (`-244,250`). For historical comparison, pre-pandemic 20-year averages stood near 345,000 for initial claims and 2.9 million for continuing claims.

On an unadjusted basis before seasonal smoothing, applications climbed 16.2 percent (`206,982`), outpacing the 11.0 percent seasonal rise models anticipated for early November. State-level Labor Department tables showed the largest weekly increases in New Jersey (`+3,564`), Missouri (`+3,029`), and Minnesota (`+2,831`), partially offset by a 2,996 decline in Michigan.

Overall U.S. job security has stayed unexpectedly firm throughout 2026: employers across private industry, nonprofits, and government have added an average of 86,000 payroll jobs per month this year — including a brisk 165,000 gain last month — marking a sharp rebound from 2025's sluggish 9,700 monthly hiring pace.

## Why did 10-year Treasury yields top 5% before the Fed's December meeting?

Rather than offering bond traders relief after [hotter-than-expected October U.S. consumer inflation data](https://smartaiintel.com/articles/october-us-cpi-inflation-beats-forecasts-fed-rate-hike-bets/), Thursday's sub-200,000 layoff trend reinforced Wall Street concerns that a tight labor market will keep wage and price pressures elevated heading into the Federal Reserve's final policy meeting of the year on December 9.

Following the release, the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield pushed above the psychologically pivotal 5.00 percent mark (`5.006%`), while 30-year yields traded around 5.358 percent ahead of a closely watched U.S. Treasury 30-year bond auction Thursday afternoon. Earlier this week, Federal Reserve Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman noted that tariffs, energy costs, and double-digit price jumps across AI data-center hardware have kept core inflation running above target — leaving policymakers weighing whether another interest-rate increase is warranted if hiring and consumer demand refuse to cool.
